Keeping a cooling heat exchanger clean: when should we clean it?

Overview

What is this?

Power plants cool their equipment with river or sea water that flows through thousands of thin metal tubes. Over time a slimy layer of bacteria (biofilm) grows inside the tubes, a bit like limescale in a kettle. This layer makes cooling worse and makes the pumps work harder. Cleaning removes it, but cleaning costs money and means stopping for a few hours. This tool estimates how dirty the tubes are, how fast they are getting dirtier, and how often to clean so that the total cost is lowest while staying safe.

How it works, in four steps

  1. Measure. Sensors give the water temperature, the water flow, how well heat passes through the tubes (U) and the pressure the pumps must overcome (dP).
  2. Work out the dirt layer. From U and dP we calculate the thickness of the slime layer and its fouling resistance (Rf). See the Fouling table tab for every step.
  3. Look ahead. A model that mixes physics with a small AI predicts how the layer will grow (Forecast tab).
  4. Choose a cleaning plan. For every possible cleaning interval (1 to 90 days) we add up cleaning cost, downtime, extra pumping and lost cooling, and check the safety limits (When to clean and Cost table).

Words used on this page

Rf (fouling resistance)
How much the slime layer blocks heat. 0 = perfectly clean. The safety limit here is 3.0 × 10⁻⁴ m²K/W.
U (heat-transfer coefficient)
How well heat passes through the tubes. Clean = 2,200 W/m²K; it drops as slime builds up.
dP (pressure drop)
How much pressure the water loses going through the tubes. Clean ≈ 7.9 kPa; it rises as slime narrows the tubes. Limit 16 kPa.
Biofilm thickness
Thickness of the slime layer, in micrometres (µm). 1 µm = one-thousandth of a millimetre.
Mechanical / chemical cleaning
Mechanical: sponge balls scrub the tubes (removes 90%). Chemical: a biocide shock (removes 95%, uses 15 kg of chemical, which is limited by an environmental permit).
Forecast band
The shaded area around a prediction: the model expects the real value to fall inside it about 9 times out of 10.
UDE / ODE
The two models. ODE = physics equations only. UDE = the same physics plus a small AI correction.
Synthetic
Made by a computer simulation, not measured at a real plant.

In plain words: each row is one moment in the data. We take the sensor readings of the last 12 hours, work out the slime thickness in two independent ways (from U and from dP), average them, and turn the thickness into Rf. The last columns show the result and what it means.

    About

    Problem

    Biofilm growing inside cooling-water tubes adds thermal resistance (1/U = 1/Uclean + Rf, Rf = δ/k) and raises the pressure drop and pumping energy. The question is when to clean: cleaning costs money, downtime and (for chemical cleaning) biocide against a permit, while fouling costs heat-duty margin and pumping power, and must stay below thermal and hydraulic limits.

    Model

    A Universal Differential Equation: the generator's known physics (Ratkowsky temperature factor, lag phase, carrying capacity, shear removal, Darcy-Weisbach/Haaland hydraulics) with a small neural network g that multiplies the deposition rate. It is trained only on measured U and dP, never on Rf, from a nominal synthetic year; an ensemble of five seeds gives the uncertainty band. The decision tab re-simulates each candidate cleaning interval and adds the ensemble's probability of crossing a limit before the first cleaning.

    Limitations

    • All data is synthetic, from the project's own generator. There is no real plant data and no plant validation.
    • The UDE shares most of its structure with the generator, so the nominal test is easy; the mismatch dataset is the honest test.
    • The decision layer's first-cycle risk rule is a prototype: five members cannot resolve small probabilities, and costs come from the generator, not from the UDE.
    • Cost constants are illustrative, not plant data.
